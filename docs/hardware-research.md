# Publison DHM 89 B2 "Digital Audio Computer" — Complete Technical Dossier for DSP Emulation

## TL;DR
- The DHM 89 B2 is a 1978–79 French dual-channel digital pitch-shifter/delay that achieves pitch shifting with **no CPU** — a fixed-rate write pointer, two variable-rate moving read pointers per channel, and an analog crossfade at the splice, all in ~200 ICs of hard-wired 74LS TTL + 4000-series CMOS logic, with a custom "flying-comma" (floating-point) 16-bit converter running up to 52.91 kHz. This exact architecture is what you must model.
- The single best public technical resources are Robert Henke's PitchLoop89 write-up and the Ableton interview (the only detailed algorithmic account, including the autocorrelation "signal-informed crossfade" / "Xing" behavior), the Studio Electronics combined owner's/service manual ($40, with a color IC-identification map + block diagram but no schematics), and a community 160 MB schematics/IC data set sold on Reverb; deep circuit detail lives with a handful of named techs (Jonathan Prager in Paris, Peter Foley/Foleytronics in Cincinnati) and reverse-engineer "roginator."
- Building a clone is legally low-risk: Publison is effectively defunct, its 1978-era French patents (if any) are long expired, and circuit topology/algorithms are not protected — you must only avoid the "Publison"/"DHM 89"/"Infernal Machine" trademarks and any copying of the exact panel artwork (trade dress). Model the behavior, name it your own.

## Key Findings

**Company & people.** Publison Audio Professional was a Paris-based French manufacturer (later at 18 Avenue de la République, 93170 Bagnolet). Secondary sources give two different founder accounts. Equipboard states the company was "founded in 1978 in Paris by Bruno Dauchez and Jean-Pierre Lafont … best known for the DHM 89 — a pioneering digital stereo pitch-shifting delay introduced in 1979." However, Ableton's interview and multiple first-hand French sources identify **Peter Dean** as the founder/director (the flamboyant scat-singing demonstrator seen at AES/NAMM) and **Philippe Petitdemange** ("Petit Demange") as the technical director / "scientific head" who actually designed the DHM 89 and IM90. Ableton's write-up states plainly: "That man was Publison founder Peter Dean, who worked alongside technical director Philippe Petitdemange to create these personality-filled effects units. In 1979 Publison released the DHM 89." Petitdemange died **16 April 2019** at Vulaines-sur-Seine — his sister, posting on Audiofanzine's "Le topic PUBLISON," recounts he was killed in a robbery for his laptop/phones at the Fontainebleau–Avon station; Peter Dean died about three and a half months later. Their deaths effectively ended the company. Publison had earlier pivoted (late 1980s–90s) to DAWs and video-post consoles (Infernal Machine 90, Full-Most, Vocoder 32, Total Edimix, NewsMix, Nature Boy sampler) before winding down.

**What it is.** A "stereo digital audio computer": dual independent channels of digital delay, echo/recirculation, continuous pitch shifting (≈0.25×–2×, i.e. two octaves down to one up), reverse playback, memory-latch (freeze/loop), automatic arpeggiation, and extensive external voltage/frequency control via a rear 24-pin Amphenol connector. Used heavily at INA/GRM (Bernard Parmegiani's *La Création du Monde*), SWR Experimentalstudio, and on pop records (Cyndi Lauper *She's So Unusual*, Prince, Kraftwerk, Tangerine Dream, Siouxsie & the Banshees).

## Details

### 1. Device history and variants

- **Timeline:** DHM 89 first released 1978 (Ableton and several catalog sources say 1979); KB 2000 keyboard companion 1980 (also cited 1982). Infernal Machine 90 released 1984, produced ~5 years to ~1989. Company effectively ended ~2019 with the deaths of Petitdemange and Dean. A "went bankrupt in 1997" claim originates on Vintage Digital's brand page and conflicts with first-hand French accounts (an engineer describes an internship there "de fin 91 à juillet 92" at Bagnolet, and the family confirms the company operated with Petitdemange "still working for Publison" until his 2019 death). **Treat the 1997 bankruptcy claim as unreliable.**
- **Naming:** "DHM 89" / "DHM89-B2" / "DHM 89 B2" refer to the same base machine across revisions. "DHM 89 B" and "DHM 90" appear in the query but are not clearly documented as distinct public models; the well-documented successor is the **Infernal Machine 90 (IM90)**, a different, microprocessor-and-screen-based product (reverb + pitch + sampling, ~50 kHz/16-bit), NOT the same as the DHM 89.
- **Documented revision differences (well-sourced from seller listings/photos):**
  - **Early units:** front panel with essentially only a power button — **no long/short memory switch**, and no dedicated vibrato depth/speed pots.
  - **Later units:** added the **short/long memory switch** (divides the delay scale, enabling both longer delays and short flange/Haas effects) and **per-channel Vibrato depth & speed controls**.
  - **Expanded-memory units:** rare versions with memory expanded to give **up to ~5 seconds of delay per channel** (vs the standard ~2 s at 20 kHz / longer at reduced bandwidth). The spec sheet notes: "In option additive memories can upgrade delay up to 5000 [ms]."
  - **Mains:** built for either 110/120 V or 220/240 V operation (internal modification; units are region-specific).
  - roginator (community) stresses findings differ across serial numbers/revisions (6-knob vs 8-knob front panels; RAM part varies MB8116 vs 4116 by production year).
- **Serial numbers:** not systematically documented publicly. One data point: a short-delay unit at serial ~1959 (roginator). No published serial-range map exists.
- **KB 2000 keyboard companion:** not self-powered — draws its supplies from the DHM 89 over the multi-pin rear connector. It adds keyboard control of pitch (playing the pitch-shift ratio from a keyboard), time-stretch playback, a Reverse Synchro function, and LFO scrubbing of loop position — turning the DHM 89 + KB 2000 into what is essentially a granular sampler years before the term existed. It uses an early CMOS/SOS (sapphire-substrate) microprocessor. Because the interface is largely analog CV/gate + a TTL pitch-clock, the KB 2000 is emulatable and even replaceable by a CV breakout box; Henke built his own 2022 hardware remote for PitchLoop89 in this spirit. The INA/GRM DHM 89 B2 + KB 2000 (maintained by Jonathan Prager, "le bon docteur Prager") resides at GRM near Maison de Radio France.

### 2. Signal path and architecture

- **Converter:** custom **"flying-comma" (virgule flottante = floating-point) 16-bit** A/D and D/A, "developed specially by Publison" — NOT an off-the-shelf converter chip. Architecture = gain-ranging (floating-point exponent) around a linear DAC core with SAR-style comparator logic implemented in the machine's wired logic; it keeps *relative* accuracy constant across level (constant SNR vs level) rather than maximizing resolution only at full scale. This gives its characteristic sound and its ~90–95 dB dynamic range. Germany's *Sound & Recording* summarizes: "Die Signalverarbeitung geschieht in Stereo mit 16 Bit bei einer maximalen Sampling-Rate von 52,91 kHz (!), welche einen Frequenzgang von bis zu 20 kHz und 95 dB Dynamik ermöglicht" (16-bit stereo, max 52.91 kHz, up to 20 kHz response, 95 dB dynamic range).
- **Sample clock:** conversion time **18.9 µs → sampling frequency 52.91 kHz** (the true converter clock).
- **Bandwidth settings & memory mapping:** three selectable responses 20 Hz–5 kHz, –10 kHz, –20 kHz (+0/–3 dB). Narrower bandwidth = fewer samples/sec = longer delay. Memory ≈ **210 kbit RAM**. In 20 kHz mode the (un-pitched) delay line is ~2 s/channel per Henke; per the transcribed Publison spec sheet, "The maximum value of delays depends upon selected bandwidth: 1 200 ms for 5 KHz, 600 ms for 10 KHz, 300 ms for 20 KHz" (these are the quasi-stereo maxima; true-stereo halves them to ~600 ms at 5 kHz and ~300 ms at 10 kHz). Expanded units ~5 s.
- **True stereo vs quasi-stereo:** true stereo splits memory into two independent channels (each with own I/O, delay, pitch, crosspoints). Quasi-stereo feeds one input to both processing channels, freeing memory for longer delays AND enabling the **20 kHz** setting (only available in quasi-stereo on many units — because in true stereo the memory is halved). A "short delay" range divides the delay scale by ten for double-tracking/flanging.
- **Pre/de-emphasis:** used in delay mode to extend dynamic range/reduce distortion; **bypassed in pitch mode** (because changing pitch ratio would shift the emphasis curve and unbalance the response).
- **Pitch mechanism:** continuously varies the read position within memory — increasing delay lowers pitch, decreasing raises it (the "digital tape loop with one record head and two moving playback heads" model Henke uses). Address counters are clocked at variable rates relative to the fixed write clock. Because memory is finite, **two adjustable crosspoints** bound the region the read pointer traverses; when the moving read reaches one crosspoint it jumps to the other. Two independent playback circuits per channel are **crossfaded** at the jump to hide the discontinuity.
- **External pitch/frequency control:** each channel accepts a TTL square wave; per the manual (via Modwiggler), the control-frequency range is "25 KHz to 250 KHz (actual endpoints are: 26.455 KHz = 0.25 pitch shift, 211.64 KHz = 2.0 pitch shift, with 105.82 KHz = 1.0)." Note 105.82 kHz = exactly 2× the 52.91 kHz sample clock. Connecting an external frequency disconnects the internal pitch pot for that channel; holding the input high latches operation and mutes output (useful for keyboard note-off).
- **Crosspoint 1/2 pots:** Crosspoint 2 sets the start of the loop/pitch region, Crosspoint 1 sets the end. Setting Crosspoint 1 *higher* than Crosspoint 2 **reverses** playback direction of the selected region (tape-backwards effect). They also define memory-latch loop boundaries. Both are externally voltage-controllable (0 to +5 V).
- **Memory latch (freeze):** captures memory contents and repeats the region between the crosspoints continuously; pitch and reverse remain available on the frozen material — i.e., an early sampler/looper.
- **Signal-informed crossfade ("Xing" in PitchLoop89):** the hardware detects optimal transition points and adapts segment length so splices land on matching phase. Henke (Ableton interview, verbatim): "You apply a process called autocorrelation… And they did this back in 1978 with an extremely clever technique that doesn't even require a microprocessor, and the way it sounds is incredibly musical… I wouldn't claim that I managed to get it the same – it definitely does not sound the same." Contemporary equivalents (Eventide H949 ALG-3, various patents) do phase-similarity/zero-cross matching; the Publison achieves a comparable adaptive segment-length behavior in wired logic. **This is the single hardest thing to emulate.**
- **Octave jumps:** switching bandwidth/memory time changes the sample rate and thus the momentary playback ratios, producing the characteristic octave jumps; low sample-rate settings add high-frequency ringing/aliasing artifacts. PitchLoop89 deliberately preserves these.
- **Vibrato LFO:** dedicated per-channel (on later units, with depth & speed). Henke's re-creation offers bipolar sine or unipolar (upward-only) square, up to ±12 semitones — a reasonable guide to the original's shapes/range.
- **Feedback/echo:** recirculation feeds output back to input; because the recirculated signal passes through the pitch section, each repeat can rise/fall in pitch (arpeggiation). On the original hardware at highest sample rate the two channels' outputs are merged before returning to input (only one routing option); PitchLoop89 adds L/R, Sum, and Cross routings and puts low-cut/high-cut filters in the feedback path (applied recursively) — these are contemporary additions, not original features.
- **Rear 24-pin Amphenol connector (community pinout, per manual & roginator; treat as version-dependent):** +15 V 100 mA (external use), digital ground, frequency-controlled ratio L, memory latch (both channels, via 0 V pulse), crosspoint 1 L, crosspoint 1 R, pitch mode (both, +5 V pulse), ISS (inhibit front-panel pots), insertion out R, insertion in R/L, left input, etc. Delay time and both crosspoints accept 0–+5 V CV once the front-panel control is disconnected via the interface; pitch accepts the TTL frequency described above. Roginator noted a different pinout list than the manual on his unit — verify against the specific revision.

### 3. Circuit-level detail

- **Overall:** "entirely built in hard-wired logic (logique câblée), ~200 ICs," no CPU, because period microprocessors were too slow. Three large circuit boards. Henke (roberthenke.com), verbatim: "all functions are carried out with a mix of analog VCAs, logic gates, counters. Tons of TTL chips, 071s and 4066s are populating three large circuit boards inside."
- **The three boards (from official Publison service docs via "ottor"/Otto Ruiter):**
  - **E128 (E128B/E128G):** analog/audio + converter-adjacent board — "relatively simple schematic, easy to repair." Wiring diagram plan C116.
  - **E132 (E132B/E132G):** the digital "wired-logic" heart (timing, memory addressing, pitch/crosspoint logic) — "original and complex wired logic … difficult to explain … requires specific factory test equipment"; Publison would not field-repair it but swapped a standard exchange board. This is why units went "back to France for 6 months."
  - **E103/C103:** board wiring/test-point diagram. Only **Plan C115 (Power Supply / Analog Circuitry) is a true schematic**; C114 is the block diagram. No full schematic of the digital E132 board exists in the wild.
- **Identified ICs (community reverse-engineering; Publison sanded/dremeled all markings deliberately to prevent cloning):**
  - **RAM:** **Fujitsu MB8116** (16 kbit DRAM), ~**13 pieces** in a short-delay unit (13 × 16 kbit ≈ 208 kbit ≈ the 210 kbit spec). Some older/expanded units may use pin-compatible **4116** DRAM. NOT 2114 static RAM. RAM and RAM sockets are a key failure point (glitching/spikes).
  - **Logic:** **74LS TTL** confirmed (a chip marked "LS02" = 74LS02) and **4000-series CMOS** confirmed (a chip marked "466B" = CD4006B, an 18-stage static shift register — significant, since shift registers are natural delay/timing building blocks). Op-amps **TL071** and analog switches **CD4066** are cited by Henke as present (see quote above) — Henke's account is the source for the 071/4066 identification.
  - **VCA/crossfade chip: not publicly identified.** No source confirms a specific VCA/OTA (no SSM2024, no CA3080 confirmation). Given the "wired-logic" design, the crossfade may be partly digital; the analog part number (if any) is unknown. This is an open question — Henke's phrase "a mix of analog VCAs" confirms VCAs are used but not which.
  - The **converter DAC core / comparator** part numbers are likewise not publicly documented — consistent with a fully custom converter.
- **Power supply:** standard three-rail **±15 V analog / +5 V digital** with separate analog and digital grounds; reservoir caps include a 10,000 µF/25 V (+5 V digital) and 2× 2,200 µF/35 V (±15 V). PSU is simple and rarely the fault. Remote-connector logic swings ±8.5 V.
- **Known failure modes (reveal function & guide emulation robustness):** ribbon cables (the most common fault), leaking backup battery, shorted tantalum capacitors (~102 caps in a full recap: 42× 4.7 µF/35 V, 20× 0.47 µF/35 V, plus electrolytics), and RAM/sockets. Intermittent noise-floor rises cured by power-cycling.
- **Filters:** input anti-aliasing and output reconstruction filters exist around the converter (needed for a 52.9 kHz sampler with 5/10/20 kHz bandwidth options), plus pre/de-emphasis in delay mode; specific topologies/cutoffs/slopes are not published — the 5/10/20 kHz brick-wall-ish responses (+0/–3 dB) and the low-rate ringing are the audible targets to model.

### 5. Existing emulations and prior art

- **Robert Henke's PitchLoop89** (Max for Live; in Ableton Live 11+ Suite core library, free with Suite) — the definitive modern reinterpretation. Henke's own technology page (roberthenke.com/technology/pitchloop89.html) and the Ableton "What Goes Around Comes Around" interview and Synthtopia two-part masterclass are the richest algorithmic sources. Key modeling notes: dual independent pitch-shift delays; one record + two moving playback heads with crossfade; global Bandwidth (B/W) sets sample rate → max delay, playback values, and 20/10/5 kHz limit (2 s/channel at 20 kHz); Hold (freeze) loop set by Position + Segment; Back (reverse); Short; **Xing** (signal-informed crossfade via autocorrelation); Fade (longer crossfade, needs longer min delay); Pitch ±24 st, Fine ±1 st; Vibrato (sine bipolar / square unipolar, ±12 st); Position + Segment define the slice; Random & LFO position modulation; filters (low-cut/high-cut) inside feedback; Routing L/R–Sum–Cross; MIDI note control anchored at C3 with glide via CC#05; hidden test-oscillator easter egg. Henke explicitly kept the octave jumps and low-rate ringing for aesthetics and added the cross-feedback routing and LFOs that the hardware lacked.
- **Henke's 2022 hardware remote** (roberthenke.com/technology/pl-remote.html) drives a PitchLoop89 variant for the CBM8032AV live show — an "inspired-not-replica" approach.
- **Other prior art / standard treatments to build from:** the variable-delay dual-read-head crossfading pitch shifter is the textbook "delay-line pitch shifter." Read **Jon Dattorro, "Effect Design, Part 2: Delay-Line Modulation and Chorus," JAES 45(10):764–788, Oct 1997** (free PDF at ccrma.stanford.edu/~dattorro/EffectDesignPart2.pdf) for the sawtooth-driven variable delay + envelope/crossfade math; **Udo Zölzer (ed.), DAFX** for the modulated-delay pitch transposer with two/three delay lines and windowed crossfade (see also Disch/Zölzer DAFx99 paper dafx.de/paper-archive/1999/disch.pdf); Miller Puckette's *Theory and Technique* (msp.ucsd.edu, "Pitch shifting" node) for the two-copy overlap-add formulation; Valhalla DSP's blog on H949 de-glitching for the autocorrelation/phase-alignment approach that mirrors Xing; and katjaas.nl on low-latency cross-correlation splicing. Eventide's H910/H949 lineage is the closest hardware cousin. No open-source Reaktor/Pd/VCV clone of the specific Publison was found; PitchLoop89 is the reference implementation.

### 6. Legal considerations

- **Company/IP status:** Publison is effectively defunct (founders both deceased ~2019; legacy maintenance handed to Jonathan Prager; archives assigned to a legatee). Ownership of any residual trademark is unclear/dormant. There is no active licensor policing the market.
- **Patents:** any French/PCT patents Publison filed in the late 1970s–early 1980s are long expired (20-year term ⇒ expired ~1998–2002), so the pitch-shift and converter techniques are free to implement. Verify by an Espacenet/INPI search on the assignee/inventor names before publishing, purely for due diligence.
- **What's safe vs risky when emulating vintage hardware (general principle, well established e.g. *Sony v. Connectix* 2000; standard clone practice à la 1176 clones):**
  - **Safe:** replicating **circuit topology, DSP algorithms, transfer functions, and sonic behavior** — these are not protected by copyright and, absent a live patent, not by patent. Describing it as "inspired by" or "-style."
  - **Risky:** using the protected **trademarks** ("Publison," "DHM 89," "Infernal Machine") in your product name/branding; copying the **exact panel artwork, layout, logo, and distinctive visual "trade dress"**; and copying any **copyrighted manual text, marketing copy, or original schematic drawings** verbatim. Give your plugin an original name, an original UI, and write your own docs.
  - **Practical guidance:** model the algorithm from first principles + the public descriptions; reference the hardware honestly ("inspired by a rare 1978 French pitch-shifting delay") without implying endorsement; don't ship sampled ROM/firmware; you're free to distribute impulse/behavioral matches you derive yourself.

## Recommendations

1. **Start from PitchLoop89 as your functional spec.** Buy/instantiate it (Live 11+ Suite), and study Henke's technology page + the Ableton interview + Synthtopia masterclass. Reproduce its parameter set first (record head + two moving read heads, Position/Segment, Bandwidth→samplerate octave jumps, Hold, Back, Xing, Fade, Vibrato, Random/LFO position mod), since Henke already solved the hard mapping decisions.
2. **Buy the two paid documents** in parallel: the **Studio Electronics manual ($40)** for the block diagram, specs, test-point behavior and the color IC-identification map; and contact the **Reverb seller ("roginator," listing 15158238)** for the 160 MB IC/schematic data set. Together these de-risk the converter and logic modeling.
3. **Model the converter deliberately.** Implement a floating-point/gain-ranging quantizer (constant relative error vs level) at 52.91 kHz with selectable 5/10/20 kHz band-limiting and pre/de-emphasis in delay mode only. This "flying-comma" nonlinearity + the low-rate reconstruction ringing are core to the sound — a plain 16-bit linear PCM model will sound wrong.
4. **Invest your DSP effort in the adaptive crossfade ("Xing").** Implement autocorrelation/phase-alignment splice-point detection with adaptive segment length and a short window; expose a fallback fixed crossfade. Accept (as Henke did) that a perfect match to the hardware is unlikely — aim for musical glitches, not clinical ones.
5. **For the KB 2000 dimension,** model it as a MIDI/CV control layer over the same engine (keyboard→pitch ratio anchored so control-frequency 105.82 kHz = ratio 1, LFO loop scrubbing, time-stretch playback, reverse-sync), rather than a separate DSP object.
6. **Reach the people with the hardware** for gaps you can't close from docs: email **Peter Foley (foleytronics)** for bench-level IC/board photos and behavior, and try **Jonathan Prager** (Paris) for the authoritative maintenance perspective and the GRM unit. Post the specific open question — "what VCA/OTA performs the playback crossfade?" — on the R/E/P topic 8169 and GroupDIY 32671 threads.
7. **Legal:** name the product originally, design an original UI, write original docs, run one Espacenet/INPI patent check on "Publison/Petitdemange/Dean" for the record, and market it as "inspired by" the hardware. No licensing is required.

**Benchmarks that would change the plan:** if you obtain the E132 digital-board schematic or a full de-sanded IC bill of materials (from roginator's set or a tech), switch from behavioral modeling to circuit-accurate modeling of the address-counter/crossfade logic. If an actual Publison patent surfaces with the crossfade algorithm, implement it directly (and re-confirm expiry). If you can A/B against a serviced hardware unit (Foley/Prager/GRM), use it to tune the converter nonlinearity and Xing window.

## Caveats

- **Two conflicting founder narratives** exist. Equipboard attributes the 1978 founding to Bruno Dauchez and Jean-Pierre Lafont; Ableton and first-hand French sources credit Peter Dean (founder/showman) and Philippe Petitdemange (technical designer). The Dean/Petitdemange account is better corroborated, and Petitdemange is best treated as the **actual circuit designer** with reasonable confidence. The Dauchez/Lafont attribution may reflect corporate registration rather than the design team.
- **Dates wobble** across sources: DHM 89 release 1978 vs 1979; KB 2000 1980 vs 1982; a "bankrupt 1997" claim (Vintage Digital) conflicts with evidence of the company operating into the 2010s. I've flagged these inline.
- **The converter, crossfade VCA, and filter specifics are partly inferred.** Bit depth is "quasi-16-bit flying-comma" (floating-point PCM), confirmed by *Sound & Recording* as effectively 16-bit at up to 52.91 kHz — this is floating-point PCM, **not** delta modulation or companding as the query wondered. No public source names the crossfade VCA chip or the DAC core.
- **Pinouts vary by revision** — the 24-pin connector list and the pitch-clock endpoints are community/manual-sourced and roginator found discrepancies vs the manual; verify against the exact unit.
- **Publison reportedly "never made full schematics"** and filed off all IC markings deliberately, so a complete circuit-accurate reconstruction may be impossible without the factory exchange-board knowledge that largely died with Petitdemange. Behavioral modeling (the PitchLoop89 approach) is the realistic path.
- Community forum claims (roginator's IC IDs, pinouts, recap lists) are credible and cross-checked across multiple units but are not manufacturer-verified. The manufacturer spec figures (52.91 kHz, 18.9 µs, 210 kbit, dynamic range) come from Publison's own documentation as transcribed by Vintage Digital and corroborated by *Sound & Recording* and the French company history.
